# Culík & Yu prove membership undecidable for classes they invented, not for Wolfram's — and not for the 256 elementary rules

The paper was **reached and read in full**: all fourteen pages of the publisher's free scan of *Complex Systems* **2** (1988) 177–190, [`02-2-2.pdf`](https://content.wolfram.com/uploads/sites/13/2018/02/02-2-2.pdf), including every definition, all thirteen theorems, all three corollaries, both appendices and the reference list. Every claim below is read off that text. The scan carries an OCR layer, which I extracted and then spot-checked against the rendered page images for pages 180, 183 and 186 — the three pages that carry the load-bearing definitions, the Theorem 5 reduction and the Class Three material; the OCR matched the images on every load-bearing string. No survey, abstract, encyclopaedia entry or recollection was used for any claim about what the paper says. The claim being verified was read from the [#4 survey's own §2.3](https://github.com/NGL321/mosaic/blob/research/grokking-eca-tda-survey/docs/research/2026-07-25-grokking-eca-tda-survey.md) on `research/grokking-eca-tda-survey`, not from a paraphrase of it. What was *not* done: the cited internal report [2] (Culik–Pachl–Yu, Waterloo CS-87-47), which Theorem 1 leans on, was not retrieved, and the Theorem 12 construction is given by the authors as a sketch, so it was followed but not fully re-derived.

## 0. Verdict

> **The debt discharges, and it discharges against the survey as much as for it: class membership *is* what Culík & Yu prove undecidable, but for a hierarchy of four classes they define themselves — one of which contains every CA and so is trivially decidable — and undecidability is a statement about the infinite space of all CA descriptions, which the 256 elementary rules are not. The initial-condition-ensemble dependence is genuinely the paper's own, and is sharper than the survey's version; the *reason* the survey gives for it ("the classification is defined on evolution from disordered states") is not the paper's and contradicts it.**

## 1. What the paper actually defines, and how far it is Wolfram's

Culík & Yu do not take Wolfram's four classes as given and analyse them. They state in the second paragraph of [the paper](https://content.wolfram.com/uploads/sites/13/2018/02/02-2-2.pdf) (p. 178) that "we propose a more specific definition of these four classes of CA", and the definition they propose differs from Wolfram's in four ways that matter for anything the survey wants to do with it.

**It is a hierarchy, not a partition.** "It is convenient for the presentation of our results to define the classes as a hierarchy, i.e. a higher class includes each lower class as a subset" (p. 178). Class One ⊆ Class Two ⊆ Class Three ⊆ Class Four. Wolfram's four classes are used as a partition; these are nested. The authors note the fix is available — "it is, of course, easy to modify this definition, i.e. to define Class Two as our Class Two minus Class One" — but they do not adopt it, and the theorems are proved about the cumulative version.

**It is defined on finite configurations, relative to a chosen stable state.** A configuration is *finite* if all but finitely many cells are in a distinguished quiescent state (p. 179); an *s*-finite configuration is the same relative to any stable state *s*. Definition 1 (p. 180): a CA is in Class One "if there is a stable state *s* ∈ *S* such that all the *s*-finite configurations of *A* evolve to the homogeneous configuration of *s*." Definition 2 (p. 184) replaces "evolve to the homogeneous configuration" with "have an ultimately periodic evolution". Both quantify universally over an explicitly named ensemble and existentially over the stable state.

**Class Three is not "chaotic" and Class Four is not "complex localised structures".** Definition 3 (p. 186): a CA is in Class Three "if there exists a stable state *s* such that for any pair of *s*-finite configurations *c*₁ and *c*₂, it is decidable whether *c*₁ evolves to *c*₂. Class Four includes all cellular automata." Class Three is a *decidability* condition on the reachability relation — Theorem 11 restates it as "the descendant language of every *s*-finite configuration is recursive". Class Four is the universal class. Neither is a formalisation of the corresponding Wolfram class; they are the authors' own construction, and the only stated bridge to Wolfram's is behavioural intuition plus the agreement checked in the next paragraph.

**The agreement with Wolfram is checked on exactly one family, and it is not the elementary rules.** On p. 180 the authors verify that the totalistic k=2, r=2 rules in Class One under their definition are codes 0, 4, 16, 32, 36, 48, 54, 60, 62 and no others, and conclude "our definition of Class One agrees with Wolfram's definition for totalistic CA with k=2 and r=2. However, our definition for Class One appears to be a larger class for more complex cellular automata." That is the whole of the claimed correspondence: an exact match on the 64 totalistic radius-2 codes Wolfram used as worked examples in [7], and an admission of divergence everywhere else. The 256 elementary (k=2, r=1) rules are never mentioned in the paper.

## 2. What is proved undecidable, and by what reduction

Three membership theorems, each proved, each about a named class ([pp. 182–187](https://content.wolfram.com/uploads/sites/13/2018/02/02-2-2.pdf)).

**Theorem 5 (p. 182).** "It is undecidable whether all the finite configurations of a given cellular automaton evolve to the quiescent configuration (in linear time)." **Corollary 1 (p. 184)** reads Class One membership straight off it by taking the stable state to be the quiescent state.

The reduction is **from the emptiness problem for Turing machines**, not from halting — this is worth stating precisely because the ticket's curriculum note anticipated the halting problem. The authors flag the obstacle themselves, and the obstacle is exactly the initial-condition question: "the reduction is not straightforward due to the fact that unlike a Turing machine, a cellular automaton does not have a separate input alphabet and thus has no distinguished initial configurations. If the finite configurations of a cellular automaton *A* are used to encode the instantaneous descriptions (IDs) of *M*, then the encoding of every ID of *M* including the unreachable ones can appear as an initial configuration of *A*." The construction absorbs the problem rather than restricting the ensemble: each non-quiescent state has three components, so a segment has three tracks. Track 1 holds a candidate sequence of IDs separated by dollar signs; track 2 cycles through a cleaning phase (*n*−1 steps), a Firing Squad Synchronization phase (2*n*−2 steps) and a checking phase (*n*−1 steps) in which every cell simultaneously verifies that track 1 is a legal accepting computation history of *M*; track 3 carries the timing signal and has a form checkable by the local function in one step. Quiescence is generated and spreads if the third track is invalid, if the boundary states are missing, or if the check fails. Hence: *L*(*M*) = ∅ ⟺ no segment survives ⟺ every finite configuration reaches quiescence, and in linear time — the authors bound a surviving-then-dying segment of length *n* at 8*n*−9 steps because an initial segment may contain "junk" in track 2 in any phase.

**Theorem 8 (p. 185).** "It is undecidable whether a cellular automaton is in Class Two." Reduced from Corollary 2 (undecidability of whether *M* halts starting from *every* ID), which is itself derived from Theorem 5 — the authors note explicitly that they "have not found a proof that uses the former approach without actually mimicking the latter", i.e. the natural direct reduction from halting was not available to them.

**Theorem 9 (p. 186).** "It is undecidable whether a Class Two cellular automaton is in Class One" — the abstract's "even when choosing only between the two simplest classes". The proof is one sentence: "this has actually been proved in the proof of theorem 5." That compression is real but it does check out, and it is worth writing down because a verifier should not take a one-sentence proof on trust: in the Theorem 5 construction the segment boundaries never move and track 1 never changes while the segment survives, so a surviving configuration's orbit is confined to a finite set and is therefore ultimately periodic whatever *M* does. The constructed *A* is thus always in Class Two, and in Class One iff *L*(*M*) = ∅.

**Theorem 12 (p. 186).** "It is undecidable whether a given cellular automaton is in Class Three." Two tracks: one simulating *M* on ε, one simulating a universal machine *U*, with a signal shuttling between the boundaries. If *M* halts on ε, everything eventually goes periodic or quiescent; otherwise *A*(*M*) simulates all computations of *U*. The authors present this one as an idea plus details ("our proof is more complicated than we expected. We encourage reader to give a simpler proof"), not as a complete formal construction.

**Class Four membership is decidable, trivially.** Definition 3 makes Class Four the set of all cellular automata. The abstract's "it is undecidable to which class a given cellular automaton belongs" is therefore true of the three-way question and vacuous for the fourth class. Anyone repeating the sentence should not carry it as "all four Wolfram classes are undecidable".

Two further results bear on whether a *computable proxy* could stand in for the class label, which is what an experiment would actually reach for. **Theorem 2 (p. 182):** there is a one-dimensional Class One CA whose limit language is not regular — and by the same argument it can be non-context-free "or even a language of any given time or space complexity". The authors draw the moral themselves: "It has been suggested that the limit languages are related to the classification. The above shows that there is no such simple relation." **Theorem 1 (p. 181)** shows the four conditions *all configurations reach a homogeneous configuration*, *they do so in bounded (constant) time*, *all s-finite configurations do so in bounded time*, and *the limit set is the single homogeneous configuration* are equivalent to each other — and then observes that "none of the above statements is implied by the fact that a cellular automaton is in Class One". So the natural bounded-time proxy is strictly stronger than the class, not an approximation of it.

## 3. Where the ensemble of initial conditions enters, and where it does not

The survey's sentence is: "**class assignment depends on the set of initial conditions used**, because the classification is defined on evolution from disordered states" ([§2.3, finding 2](https://github.com/NGL321/mosaic/blob/research/grokking-eca-tda-survey/docs/research/2026-07-25-grokking-eca-tda-survey.md)). The two halves have different fates.

**The dependence is the paper's own claim, and the paper states it three separate ways.** First structurally: every class definition is relativised to an ensemble (*s*-finite configurations) and to an existentially quantified stable state *s*, so a CA with two stable states has two candidate verdicts and is in the class if either works. Second by counterexample — Example 2 (p. 180): "The following (infinite) configuration of the Class One cellular automaton with k=2, r=2 and function code 16 never evolves to a homogeneous configuration: …0101010101…", introduced with the remark that "a Class One cellular automaton, **by either Wolfram's or our definition**, can have 'exceptional' configurations that do not evolve to a homogeneous configuration". That is a named rule whose class label survives only because the ensemble excludes that configuration, and the authors attribute the phenomenon to Wolfram's definition as well as their own. Third by theorem — Theorem 1 plus the note after it establishes that quantifying over *all* configurations instead of the finite ones is not a refinement of Class One but a strictly stronger and differently-behaved condition. The dependence is therefore not a later gloss; it is load-bearing structure, and the paper is sharper about it than the survey is.

**The stated reason is not the paper's, and points the wrong way.** Culík & Yu classify on **finite** configurations — all but finitely many cells quiescent — which is the *opposite* of a disordered state, and they say so as a deliberate choice: "we will classify CA by their behavior on finite configurations even if the computations on all possible configurations might be of interest" (p. 178). The words *disordered*, *random*, *ensemble*, *measure*, *typical* and *sample* do not occur in the paper in that role. The single acknowledgement of the statistical reading is in the abstract, describing what they are replacing: Wolfram "proposed a classification of these automata based on their statistically observed behavior". Section 6, the one place the paper touches a Wolfram observation about perturbations — single-site difference propagation, from [9] — recovers it as a corollary of Theorem 13 on products of automata, not from any ensemble argument. So the two senses have to be kept apart: **quantifier-domain dependence** (which ensemble the universal quantifier ranges over: finite, *s*-finite, or all) is Culík & Yu's; **statistical-ensemble dependence** (which random initial states you sampled, and with what measure) is a real property of Wolfram-style empirical classification but is nowhere in this paper, and attributing it here is the gloss.

## 4. Why none of this reaches the 256 elementary rules

Undecidability is a property of a decision problem, and a decision problem needs an infinite input domain to have one. What Theorem 5, Theorem 8 and Theorem 12 establish is that there is no algorithm that takes an *arbitrary CA description* — arbitrary state set, arbitrary neighbourhood, arbitrary local function — and returns its class. The witnessing automata in the proofs are large: three-track and two-track constructions with state sets built from Turing-machine IDs, boundary markers and Firing-Squad phase symbols. Nothing in the paper constrains them to small alphabets, and nothing in the paper is about k=2, r=1.

For any *fixed finite* family of rules — the 256 elementary rules being the case at hand — membership in Culík & Yu's Class One, Two or Three is a finite relation, hence decidable by table lookup, whatever the difficulty of *filling in* the table. The survey's phrase "conventional labels on a **provably undecidable predicate**" is therefore not licensed by this source: the predicate restricted to the object the survey cares about is not undecidable, and the paper proves nothing about that restriction. This is the one place where the [#4 survey](https://github.com/NGL321/mosaic/blob/research/grokking-eca-tda-survey/docs/research/2026-07-25-grokking-eca-tda-survey.md) overreaches its source, and it overreaches it in the direction of its own conclusion, which is the direction that matters.

The honest version of the worry is available *from inside the paper*, and is better evidence than the undecidability theorem for the survey's actual purpose. On p. 186 the authors report that among the totalistic k=2, r=2 rules they can place codes 2, 6, 10, 12, 14, 18, 22, 26, 28, 30, 34, 38, 42, 44, 46 and 50 in Class Three by an expansion argument, and then: "However, we are unable to show that the ones with function code 20 and 52 are or are not in Class Three." Two concrete small rules, in the very family where their definition is claimed to agree with Wolfram's, left unclassified by the people who wrote the classification. Example 4 (p. 184) adds that code 52 is not in Class Two, witnessed by a specific 24-bit configuration. That is a fact about small rules, not about the space of all CAs, and it is what a categorisation experiment should actually be told.

## 5. What survives for the survey's finding

The survey's belief under test was "the well-characterised rule taxonomy makes ECA uniquely suited to compositionality and categorisation experiments", and its verdict — that the taxonomy is not well-characterised in the sense the rung needs — survives, but on a reorganised and weaker basis than [the text it currently rests on](https://github.com/NGL321/mosaic/blob/research/grokking-eca-tda-survey/docs/research/2026-07-25-grokking-eca-tda-survey.md).

What Culík & Yu genuinely supply: (i) Wolfram's classes as stated are informal enough that formalising them is a research contribution, and the formalisation the authors chose is admittedly non-unique, cumulative rather than partitional, and divergent from Wolfram's outside one worked family — a taxonomy whose formal content is contested is not "well-characterised" in any useful sense; (ii) there is no uniform decision procedure for the classes, so class labels can never be *derived* by algorithm, only asserted, checked case by case, or observed empirically; (iii) the natural computable proxies do not track the classes — limit languages provably do not (Theorem 2), bounded-time convergence is strictly stronger (Theorem 1); (iv) the class label is relative to a declared ensemble and a declared stable state, with a named counterexample rule (code 16) showing what changes when the ensemble does; (v) two named small rules were left unclassified by the authors themselves.

What it does not supply, and what the survey should stop claiming from it: that the *Wolfram* classes were formalised and shown undecidable; that all four classes are undecidable; that the 256-rule labelling rests on an undecidable predicate; and that the ensemble dependence arises because classification is defined on disordered initial states. The corrected version of (iv) is stronger for experimental design than the version currently in the survey, because it names the free parameter precisely — the quantifier's domain and the choice of stable state — rather than gesturing at sampling.

## What this does not establish

### Sources not reached

Two of the paper's own supports were not opened. Theorem 1's proof defers the equivalence of its first two statements to "corollary 2 of [2]" — Culik, Pachl & Yu, *On the Limit Sets of Cellular Automata*, Research Report CS-87-47, University of Waterloo (1987) — an unpublished internal report I did not retrieve, so the bounded-time equivalence in Theorem 1 is accepted here on the authors' citation rather than verified. Theorem 2 similarly cites Hurd's *Complex Systems* 1 (1987) 69–80 for the existence of a CA with a non-regular limit language, which I did not open; the Theorem 2 construction on top of it is elementary and was checked. Wolfram's [7] (*Physica* 10D, 1984) and [9] (*Physica Scripta* T9, 1985) were not read for this document, so every statement here about what Wolfram's classes are is a statement about *how Culík & Yu describe them*, not an independent reading of Wolfram — which is the correct scope for this ticket but is a real limit on §1's comparison. Nothing was blocked or paywalled; these were scope decisions.

### Open gaps

The arithmetical degree of the classification problems is not settled here and is not settled by the paper: Culík & Yu prove undecidability and do not locate Class One, Two or Three membership in the arithmetical hierarchy, though Theorem 5's reduction from TM emptiness gives Π₁-hardness of Class One membership for free. Whether a *partitional* version of the hierarchy — the "Class Two minus Class One" variant the authors mention and decline — inherits all three undecidability results was not checked and does not follow immediately for the top of the hierarchy, since Class Four minus Class Three is the complement of a Σ-condition. Whether any of the constructed witnessing automata can be pushed down to small alphabets, which is what would be needed before undecidability said anything about rule spaces the size of the elementary rules, is untouched by the paper and by me. And the status of totalistic k=2, r=2 codes 20 and 52 — open in 1988 — was not checked against later literature, since this ticket is about what this paper establishes.

### Load-bearing ifs

Verdict 5 depends on the claim that the paper never treats k=2, r=1 rules: I searched the full text and found only k=2, r=2 totalistic examples, but the argument would weaken if a radius-1 construction is hiding in a formula the OCR dropped — the extraction lost several display equations on pp. 179 and 188, and I verified images only for pp. 180, 183 and 186. Verdict 7 depends on the absence of any discussion of disordered or random initial states, which is a negative claim about a fourteen-page text; I read all fourteen pages, but a negative claim of that shape is exactly what an adversarial reader should re-check first. Verdict 3 depends on reading Definition 3's "Class Four includes all cellular automata" as constitutive rather than as a remark about containment — the sentence sits inside Definition 3 and the hierarchy framing supports the constitutive reading, but if Class Four were instead intended as a residual class the triviality claim fails. And §2's reconstruction of Theorem 9 depends on the segment boundaries never moving in Theorem 5's construction; the text says track 1 "remains unchanged" and describes fixed boundary states but does not say boundaries are immobile in so many words, so that is my inference from the construction, not a quotation.

The paper never treats k=2, r=1 rules. The directly usable evidence is smaller and better: among the totalistic k=2, r=2 rules, Culík & Yu could place sixteen codes in Class Three and reported that they were "unable to show that the ones with function code 20 and 52 are or are not in Class Three" (p. 186). Published class assignments for the 256 elementary rules are conventional and mostly agreed, so an experiment can use them as labels — but they are **conventions, not derivations**, and a claim of the form "the network forms a compressed representation aligned with Wolfram class" is partly a claim about the convention. That has to be stated up front, together with the ensemble the labels assume, or the result is unfalsifiable in a hidden way.
